# Techies — Brand Guide

**Version 1.2 · 6 October 2026**
For organisers, writers, designers, community partners, and anyone making something with the Techies name on it.

> A 3rd space for devs to geek out.

![Techies wordmark](https://apactechies.vercel.app/techies-logo-cropped.png)

## 1. The brand in one minute

Techies is a community where developers, dev-adjacents, and technically curious people get into how things are actually built. Real stacks. Real constraints. Real mistakes. Conversations that go beyond the demo—and often continue over supper.

**Our promise:** technical depth, without the fluff or sales pitch.

**Our personality:** curious, direct, cheeky, practical, welcoming.

**Our central tension:** serious about the craft; relaxed with the people.

**The test:** does this help someone understand a decision, ask a better question, or feel welcome in the room?

### How to read this guide

This guide draws from the existing website, linked Luma events, published recaps, and inspected artwork. The user-supplied Roast My TechStack and Start Right images are the current visual references for those events, superseding the earlier Roast campfire poster. The refreshed parent-brand direction is orange, near-black, and white/off-white. The original website's indigo and teal are historical references, not the refreshed site's primary palette. Quoted language and legacy website colour values are existing references. Mission wording, operating principles, naming conventions, layout specifications, and sample copy below are proposed standards distilled from those references, rather than previously approved policy. Visual examples are existing collateral, not newly designed templates.

### Who we are for

- **Developers and technical leads:** architecture, bottlenecks, production complexity, and engineering trade-offs.
- **Solo builders and founders:** decisions made under real constraints, including AI-assisted building.
- **Dev-adjacents:** PMs, designers, and others whose work intersects with product and engineering.
- **Technically curious people:** willing to engage with the details, regardless of title or seniority.

Depth is the bar for the conversation, not a credential required at the door. Explain necessary context without turning everything into a beginner tutorial.

### Positioning statement

For builders who want more than polished demos and surface-level talks, Techies is a third space for candid technical conversations. Through community demos, incident discussions, and hands-on workshops, we examine how things are built, why decisions were made, and what could work better.

## 3. Core values, expressed through behaviour

The website explicitly names **Real Conversations**, **Deep Technical**, and **High Intensity**. The following turns those pillars—and the community practices shown on Luma—into usable principles.

| Value | What it means | What it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Real conversations over polished pitches** | Share the working reality, including the awkward parts. | Presenters explain constraints and current pain points. Partner acknowledgements are clear and separate from technical discussion. |
| **Depth over fluff** | Go beyond what a product does into how and why it works. | Ask about architecture, alternatives, bottlenecks, costs, failure modes, and trade-offs. |
| **Curiosity over ego** | Challenge decisions without humiliating the person who made them. | “Why this approach?” instead of “Who would build it like that?” The roast is cheeky technical debate, not a personal takedown. |
| **Practical learning over hype** | Make the conversation useful to someone building tomorrow. | Discuss recovery steps, guardrails, shortcuts, and lessons with enough context to apply them. |
| **Community beyond the agenda** | The people matter as much as the programme. | Invite people to supper, make space for questions, and recognise the volunteers keeping the room running. |

**High intensity means engaged discussion and probing questions.** It does not mean aggression, exclusion, or theatrical outrage.

## 4. Voice and writing style

### The voice

Write like a technically competent friend inviting you into a good engineering conversation. Be specific enough to earn trust and human enough to make people want to show up.

| Quality | Do | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct | Lead with the actual question or problem. | Long scene-setting and marketing introductions. |
| Technically grounded | Name the decision, constraint, or failure mode. | “Innovation,” “disruption,” and “cutting-edge” without substance. |
| Curious | Ask “What breaks first?” or “Why not the alternative?” | Declare one stack universally right. |
| Cheeky | Use recognisable developer humour sparingly. | Forced memes, constant profanity, or jokes at a participant’s expense. |
| Welcoming | Invite people to compare approaches and ask questions. | “Real engineers only” or seniority-based gatekeeping. |
| Honest | Say what the session covers and what is still unresolved. | Guaranteed outcomes, invented metrics, or exaggerated claims. |

### Existing voice anchors

These are source phrases, useful for calibrating tone:

> “AI can fix your code. It cannot fix a bad architecture decision.” — Roast My TechStack #9

> “There is no actual roasting. Just curious questions, technical debate and cheeky energy.” — Roast My TechStack #9

> “Business decisions shape architecture. Architecture shapes what the business can do.” — Building Products: Starting Right

> “Come for the event. Stay for the company.” — Website

### Writing rules

1. **Start with the substance.** A decision, a failure, a question, or a clear invitation.
2. **Use concrete nouns and active verbs.** “Compare recovery strategies” beats “unlock transformative insights.”
3. **Explain the mechanism.** Name what participants will present, discuss, or work through.
4. **Keep paragraphs short.** One idea per paragraph; bullets for format and logistics.
5. **Use “we” and “you.”** Sound like people hosting people.
6. **Use technical language when it earns its place.** Spell out less familiar acronyms on first use for mixed audiences.
7. **Use British/Singapore English consistently:** organise, optimise, programme; preserve code and product names verbatim.
8. **Make dates unambiguous:** `3 Sep 2026 · 6:30–9:30 pm SGT`. Include the year and timezone for online or regional audiences.
9. **Keep punctuation purposeful.** Preserve `Prod is DOWN!!!` as an event title; ordinary copy does not need repeated exclamation marks.
10. **Keep the edge situational.** Developer humour fits incident stories and community channels. Partner notes and logistics should stay clear and neutral.

“No fluff” is a promise to demonstrate, not a phrase to repeat in every paragraph. The website’s sharper criticism of marketing events is a tone reference; new copy should spend more time showing our depth than attacking other communities.

### Before and after

| Generic copy | Techies copy |
| --- | --- |
| Join industry leaders for an inspiring evening of innovation. | Bring your architecture questions. We’re getting into the decisions behind the demo. |
| Discover best practices for robust systems. | What broke? How did they recover? What would have stopped it? |

| Unlock the power of AI-driven development. | Where does AI help your build—and where does it make the wrong decision faster? |
| This architecture is obviously terrible. | What constraint made this architecture the right choice at the time? |

### Tone by context

- **Event invitations:** punchy hook, concrete format, warm invitation.
- **Technical recaps:** precise, structured, restrained. Separate what was demonstrated, suggested, and left unresolved. Attribute observations to speakers.
- **Incident sessions:** candid and empathetic. Laugh at familiar situations, not someone’s loss or distress.
- **Partner communications:** practical and respectful. State each partner’s role accurately.
- **Community updates:** informal and useful. One clear action; minimal ceremony.
- **Logistics:** plain language. Never hide important details inside jokes.

## 6. Visual identity

### Design idea: a technical room with human warmth

The website supplies the stable layer: near-black surfaces, clear typography, a terminal-style wordmark, and distinct event accents. Event artwork supplies personality. Real community photography supplies warmth and belonging.

Keep those roles distinct. The whole brand does not need to look like an outage poster, and the whole event family does not need identical artwork.

### Logo

**Existing primary asset:** [Techies wordmark PNG](https://apactechies.vercel.app/techies-logo-cropped.png), a white `>Techies_` mark.  
**Existing event asset:** [Roast pixel flame PNG](https://apactechies.vercel.app/roast-logo.png).

**Current collateral treatment:** the supplied Roast and Start Right artwork uses a cream-and-orange `by >Techies_` attribution. Preserve that treatment within those compositions; the white website wordmark remains the standalone parent-brand asset. The standalone pixel flame is a legacy supporting asset, while the current Roast cover uses a flame rising from an isometric stack.

Recommended usage rules:

- Preserve the leading `>` and trailing `_`, proportions, and original artwork.
- Use the white wordmark on a dark, quiet field. Add a dark panel over photography when needed.
- Leave clear space of at least one capital-T height on all sides.
- Start at 140 px wide for digital use; check the actual export at its intended size. Do not shrink the full wordmark into an illegible avatar.
- Use the supplied image for brand lockups. A live-text `>Techies_` treatment may be used in website navigation, as the reference site does; it is not an exact replacement for the artwork.
- Keep the flame associated with Roast My TechStack, not as a replacement for the parent Techies logo.
- Do not stretch, outline, add shadows to, or arbitrarily recolour supplied marks. Preserve the existing orange attribution in the supplied event artwork. A light-background variant needs its own prepared asset.

### Colour palette

**Refreshed parent brand: orange, near-black, and white/off-white.** Use orange for primary actions, links, focus states, and restrained highlights. Black/charcoal surfaces and off-white type carry most of the page.

The neutral values below retain the reference website's CSS. `#F97316` is the selected orange token for the refreshed website, not a sampled or claimed exact match to the supplied posters. Preserve the original artwork colours within those assets.

| Colour | Hex | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Near-black | `#0A0A0F` | Main background |
| Deep charcoal | `#111118` | Secondary sections |
| Card charcoal | `#16161F` | Cards and panels |
| Border grey | `#252530` | Decorative dividers |
| Off-white | `#E8E8ED` | Headings and primary text |
| Secondary grey | `#9898A8` | Supporting text on dark backgrounds |
| Muted grey | `#686878` | Reference token; avoid for essential small text |
| Techies orange | `#F97316` | Parent-brand actions, links, focus states, and highlights |
| Orange hover | `#EA580C` | Hover state for orange-filled actions |
| Incident red | `#EF4444` | Prod is DOWN!!! event-specific accents; not a parent-brand action colour |

**Recommended balance:** mostly dark neutrals, generous off-white text, and orange as the dominant website accent. Use near-black text on orange-filled buttons. Avoid purple/indigo and teal in the parent-brand interface, including code decoration. Keep the standalone wordmark asset white and unchanged.

**Legacy website reference only:** indigo `#6366F1`, Roast amber `#F59E0B`, and Brewing teal `#2DD4BF` appeared in the old website's CSS. They explain the previous implementation; they do not override the refreshed orange/black/off-white direction. Event artwork may retain its own established colours, including incident red and Start Right's blue diagrams.

**Current event collateral palette:** use the supplied images as the colour reference for near-black navy backgrounds, graphite-metal structures, cream titles, orange labels, and orange/amber illumination. Start Right adds blue diagram lines. These are observed artwork colours, not verified hex values. Keep this event palette distinct from the website tokens: do not replace the supplied orange with web amber or force teal into Start Right.

For essential text, target WCAG AA contrast: 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Check actual foreground/background combinations, particularly text over images. Use near-black text on orange fills, including their hover states. Never rely on colour alone for event identification, errors, or interaction states. Subtle border colours are decorative; interactive controls still need visible boundaries and focus states.

### Typography

**Existing web fonts:** Inter and JetBrains Mono.

| Role | Typeface | Recommended treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main headlines | Inter | 700–800; compact, clear; sentence case |
| Body and logistics | Inter | 400–500; comfortable line height |
| Buttons and subheadings | Inter | 600–700 |
| Code, labels, edition numbers | JetBrains Mono | 400–600; short passages |

For web work, begin with 16–18 px body text, 1.6 line height, 28–36 px section headings, and 40–64 px desktop hero headings. Let headings scale down on mobile. Keep long-form reading columns around 65–80 characters wide.

The current Roast and Start Right covers use heavy uppercase sans-serif headlines in cream, smaller widely spaced orange labels, and compact Techies attribution. Use that hierarchy for new collateral in these two families. Their exact artwork typefaces have not been identified; Inter 800 is the existing web-font starting point for new layouts, not a claimed exact match. Prod is DOWN!!! retains its event-specific distressed display lettering. Keep logistics in a clean sans serif and reserve display treatments for short titles.

### Layout and graphic language

- Use a clear grid, generous space, and one obvious reading order.
- Carry over the website’s 12 px standard radius and 20 px larger-card radius where suitable.
- Use code labels, terminal prompts, stack diagrams, and error motifs when they support the subject.
- Use one strong metaphor per piece: flame, incident, or product-building blocks.
- For Roast and Start Right, use a shared isometric architecture language: graphite-metal blocks, orange light, dark navy negative space, and bold cream titles. Differentiate Roast with stacked layers and a pixel flame; differentiate Start Right with exploded diagram layers and a glowing central cube.
- Keep code snippets meaningful. A playful object describing an event fits the existing voice; random code wallpaper does not add technical depth.
- Use light texture for event personality, not over small text or important details.
- For motion, use restrained transitions and respect reduced-motion preferences.

## 8. Collateral specifications

These are recommended working formats, not claims about platform upload requirements. Resize deliberately for each channel rather than stretching a square poster.

| Deliverable | Working size | Required content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reusable Luma / series cover | 1080 × 1080 px | Event name, central illustration, Techies attribution; partner mark where applicable |
| Dated square social post | 1080 × 1080 px | Event name, edition where applicable, date/venue, Techies attribution, one CTA |
| Portrait social post | 1080 × 1350 px | Hook, event identity, logistics, one CTA |
| Story | 1080 × 1920 px | Short hook, key details, clear link area; keep essentials away from interface overlays |
| Website / link preview | 1200 × 630 px | Event identity, one visual, short supporting line |
| Presentation cover | 1920 × 1080 px | Event name, speaker/session title, date, Techies and relevant partner marks |
| Recap carousel | 1080 × 1350 px per slide | Cover; constraints; key decisions; lessons; recap/event CTA |

### Recommended event poster structure

For reusable series covers, follow the supplied images: central illustration → short orange label → large cream title → optional descriptor → Techies attribution. Keep dates, venues, and registration in accompanying text. For dated promotional variants, use the structure below.

1. **Identity:** event name and edition; the largest text.
2. **Hook:** one decision or learning promise.
3. **Visual:** one relevant metaphor or photograph.
4. **Details:** date, time, timezone, and venue—or the accurate approval/disclosure note.
5. **Attribution:** Techies mark plus correctly labelled partner credit.
6. **Action:** one short URL or QR code for standalone assets, with a written link alongside the image in the post.

Start with roughly 5% outer margins. On a 1080 px square, begin around 72–110 px for the main title and 32–40 px for essential details, then check the composition at phone size. If it fails, remove copy before shrinking text. Keep critical content away from edges and preview crops.

Export social artwork in sRGB as PNG or high-quality JPEG. Retain editable masters. Place logos and event text as separate layout elements, not baked into generated background art. Keep title, date, registration details, and venue in accompanying text so the image is not the only source of information.

### Partner lockups

Use explicit labels such as **Venue partner**, **Community partner**, or **Supported by**, based on the actual arrangement. Keep partner marks separate and undistorted. Match optical prominence rather than forcing identical widths. Follow supplied partner asset rules, and retain After Dark or Tinker Time branding only where those programmes apply.

### Asset handling

For future collateral, use descriptive names such as `techies-roast-09-luma-1080x1080.png` and `techies-prod-down-03-recap-01.jpg`. Retain the event, edition, creator/credit, and source with the editable master. The links in this guide point to live reference assets; they are not a locally archived production asset pack.

## 9. Ready-to-use writing patterns

The following is new sample copy in the recommended voice. Bracketed details must be filled with confirmed event information.

### Event invitation: Roast My TechStack

> **Your demo works. What breaks first?**
>
> Bring the stack, the constraints, and the decision you’re stuck on. At Roast My TechStack, builders walk us through how their product works, then the room gets into alternatives, bottlenecks, and trade-offs.
>
> Curious questions. Technical debate. No personal takedowns.
>
> **Format:** [confirmed presentation and Q&A timings]  
> **When:** [date · time · timezone]  
> **Where:** [venue / disclosure note]
>
> [Join the session — registration link]
>
> Supper afterwards? You’re welcome to join.

### Event invitation: Prod is DOWN!!!

> **Everything was fine. Then production wasn’t.**
>
> We’re unpacking a real incident: what failed, how the team contained the damage, and the guardrails they wish they’d had.
>
> Bring your questions about blast radius, recovery, and what happens when an AI agent gets involved.
>
> [Confirmed format, date, time, venue, and registration link]

### Workshop invitation

> **What should you build—and what can wait?**
>
> Work through a product scenario from both the business and engineering sides. Compare approaches, challenge assumptions, and see how an MVP shortcut can shape the architecture later.
>
> Bring your reasoning. We’ll explore the trade-offs together.
>
> [Confirmed format, date, time, venue, and registration link]

### Recap structure

**Title:** `[Event] #[edition] · [date] · [city]`

1. What was built or what happened.
2. Constraints that shaped the decisions.
3. Architecture or incident sequence.
4. Trade-offs and alternatives discussed.
5. Practical takeaways, attributed where appropriate.
6. Open questions and next steps.
7. Speaker links, volunteer credits, and partner acknowledgements.

Do not turn a speaker’s proposed next step into a completed implementation. The published Roast #7 recap is a strong structural reference: product constraints first, concrete mechanisms next, known weak points included.

## 10. Final review

- Is the technical subject clear before the promotional language?
- Does the copy invite curiosity without insulting anyone?
- Is there a real decision, constraint, lesson, or useful question?
- Are titles, dates, venue details, links, and partner roles accurate?
- Does the piece look like Techies while retaining its event’s character?
- Are the wordmark, title, and essential details readable on a phone?
- Are images described, contrast checked, and important information available as text?
- Is there one clear next action?
